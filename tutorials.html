Dear Second Year Physicists,

As part of your practical work for this year, you are supposed to give a brief talk (maximum 20 minutes, including questions) on a subject of your choice. You organise and run the show, and I listen, comment, evaluate, and (hopefully) enjoy the performance. Here is, roughly, how it works.

You can talk just about anything, as long as it is interesting. I do not have any specific suggestions — just google “physics breakthrough of the year” or the like, and see what you get. For guidance on style and delivery, take a look at TED. You can also check who talked about what in the past, in the archive below.

Looking forward to seeing you soon. Artur

Essentially, anything that will keep me and others awake for fifteen to twenty minutes is fine. It does not have to be physics. I welcome any wacky topics.

This year’s talks

  1. Limit Cycles: Why Nature Loves Repetition

    Paulius Kalinauskas

    Many systems found in our world exhibit stable, repeating behaviour, from the rhythm of the human heart to oscillations in ecological populations and neural networks. These phenomena are often governed by mathematical structures known as limit cycles. In this talk, I introduce the concept of limit cycles within dynamical systems and explore how they provide a unifying framework for understanding such behaviour across disciplines. I will examine limit cycles in biology, physics, and even sports, to demonstrate how these mathematical objects show up in seemingly unrelated complex systems.

  2. Modelling Disinformation through Statistical Mechanics

    Ethan Kelvin

    I will aim to present a view of how to model disinformation within society using methods from statistical mechanics - specifically, the Mean Field Approach. I will begin by explaining what disinformation is and the harmful effects it can have on wider society. I will then briefly discuss earlier models of disinformation from epidemiology, before beginning a more advanced discussion, explaining the mathematics of a different statistical approach. I will then comment on the effectiveness of this model, what needs to be done to improve and validate it, and any insights we can gain about how to effectively combat this issue.

  3. Discrete Calculus and Differential Equations

    Sebastian Maslanek

    I will look into the world of discrete analogues of calculus and differential equations. I will start by looking into umbral calculus, its rules, and its interesting relation to real calculus that we are used to. Then I will use these ideas to solve the discrete version of differential equations - recurrence relations. I will then be able to make and solve the discrete versions of all of our favourite differential equations.

  4. From Pollen to Pixels: A Kinetic-Theory Intuition for Diffusion Models and Image Generation

    Sophie Neville

    Brownian motion is a classic example of microscopic randomness, so it is an unexpected place to look for the logic behind modern image generation. Diffusion models sit behind a large fraction of the generative AI people now see day to day: they create images from text prompts, edit images by filling in missing regions, and clean up noisy data. This talk will outline how diffusion models deliberately wash out structure by gradually adding noise until images become statistically simple, then explain what changes in the reverse direction: not a violation of thermodynamics, but the injection of learned information. I will describe how a neural network is trained to provide small, noise-dependent corrections that steer each step back toward realistic images, turning an irreversible-looking diffusion process into a practical generative method. I will finish by briefly discussing why the same framework is useful beyond making pictures, including scientific reconstruction problems where you want plausible solutions consistent with noisy, partial measurements.

  5. Guidance, control and reaching orbit

    Deevam Sharma

    How does a rocket get its payload to orbit? In this talk, I will explain the principles of the systems that calculate and correct the trajectories of rockets and introduce the mathematics of guidance laws and feedback control. The talk will follow a historical development beginning with early ICBMs in the 1950s up to the modern algorithms on the Space Shuttle, showing how the implementation of these algorithms subject to the technical constraints of the time influenced their development.

  6. Between a Rock and a Hard Place: the Physics of Curling

    Tabitha Shearer

    The sport of curling has been around for hundreds of years and has confused scientists for just as long, with still no consensus on how the action of curling occurs. In this talk I will provide a brief introduction to the sport and explain how experimental observations of curling motion cannot be explained by simple symmetric friction models. I will then present the leading competing theories on the motion of curling rocks and evaluate which description best matches the real world.

  7. A Numerical Algorithm for Solving Leaky Waveguide Modes

    Haotian Wang

    Leaky modes determine radiation loss and effective interaction length in plasma waveguides used for laser–plasma accelerators and compact light sources. These modes are characterized by complex propagation constants, whose imaginary part quantifies attenuation, yet computing them in realistic waveguides with arbitrary refractive index profiles remains a challenging open-boundary eigenvalue problem. In this work, we develop a robust numerical framework that solves the radial wave equation using the high-accuracy Numerov method and enforces the leaky-mode radiation condition through a two-dimensional search in the complex propagation-constant plane, combining coarse scanning with Nelder–Mead optimization to efficiently locate the complex roots. The results agree well with published data, and additional physical verification confirms the reliability of the approach, providing a practical tool for analyzing and optimizing open plasma waveguide systems.

  8. The Cosmic Distance Ladder

    Eleanor Wheeler

    How do we measure distance without rulers or maps? In this talk I will explain the construction of the cosmic distance ladder, the chain of methods astronomers use to calculate the distances to celestial objects. I will discuss the calibration of ‘standard candles’, how errors propagate through the ladder, and why accurate distance measurements are crucial to our understanding of cosmology.

  9. Geometrical and Dynamical Approaches to Special Relativity

    Noah Yudkin

    In this talk I will introduce physicists to the philosophy of special relativity, motivate the importance of the debate between the geometrical and dynamical approaches, and discuss the arguments on both sides. I will address the lack of clarity in the literature and argue that a geometrical approach serves not just philosophy but also physics.

  6. Investigating Survivorship Bias in the Formation of our Solar System

    Louis Macro

  7. Physics in Ancient China

    Qianyu Wang

    The ancient Chinese exhibited forward-thinking vision and acumen in their observations of physical phenomena. Heliocentric models, adeptly accounting for lunar eclipses, surfaced 1400 years prior to Copernicus's formulation of Heliocentrism. Moreover, explanations for camera obscura and the imaging of concave mirrors were advanced a century ahead of Euclidean geometric optics. It is noteworthy that Niels Bohr, acknowledging the philosophy of 'opposites are complementary,' adorned taijitu on his coat upon receiving chivalry in 1947. Additionally, the application of empirical rules to fluid dynamics was a forte of ancient Chinese scholars. A 60-word mnemonic facilitated the maintenance of an irrigation system benefitting over 8000 square kilometers for 2200 years. In this presentation, I shall delve into the realm of ancient Chinese physics, offering a brief analysis of its developmental trajectory.

  8. A surprising connection between prime numbers and geometry

    Louis Kemp

    Over 2000 years ago ancient Greek mathematicians wanted to know the answer to a seemingly straightforward question: “Which polygons can be constructed with a ruler and a compass?” This question remained unsolved for many years until a few brilliant insights by the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss led to a surprising result. During the talk, I will discuss the Greeks' achievements on this problem and their shortcomings. Then I will show how this problem of pure geometry can be transformed into an algebraic problem with a known solution in the most visual and hopefully intuitive way possible. This will involve us creating a new field and then trying to find out whether complex roots of a polynomial are members of this field. From this, it will turn out that a polygon can only be constructed if it has a number of sides equal to a product of Fermat primes and 2^n. A result which on first inspection is entirely unexpected.

  1. Non-Euclidean Geometry in Relativity

    Theodor Iosif

    Non-euclidean geometry is the mathematics that analyses any space which does not obey the axioms of the standard, euclidian one. This implies an extraordinary difficulty in visualising such a geometry. A particular difficulty arises in Relativity, and the aim of this talk is to illustrate the peculiarity of spaces governed by the axioms of General Relativity, with the help of a rather uncommon, detailed example through which one can develop an intuition for this field – a rotating ring.

  2. To land Lyosha on Luna: the secret Soviet moon program

    Nick Maslov

    While NASA directed its efforts at putting a man on the moon by the end of the decade, the Soviet Union didn't even seem to be competing. But many years later, information declassified amid Gorbachev-era reforms revealed a colossal lunar program, which had been hastily dissolved and swept under the rug after several launch failures and the successful American moon landings. What would a crewed Soviet moon mission have looked like? And why did the USSR, with its huge initial lead in the conquest of space, lose the race to put a man on the moon? My talk will be an exploration of the little-known story of the Soviet lunar program: from the early probes swinging by the moon and the first pictures of its far side (a mission accidentally helped by an American cereal company), to the increasingly desperate attempts at a crewed flight and the final showdown between an American crew and a Soviet probe, as well as the immense technical challenges, personal and political conflicts, triumphs, explosions and tragedies faced along the way.

  3. The Role of Continuity in Topology

    Agnieszka Wierzchucka

    Throughout a physicist's undergraduate education, pure maths is often put together as ‘mathematical methods’ courses, rushing through the required maths, drowning the reader in definitions and theorems. This only makes your common physicist less willing to leave their protected non-rigorous bubble. In this presentation, I will change that by taking a visual tour of quite an abstract area of maths - topology. Brought to life by Euler, topology is very similar to playing with play-doh, as it studies structures preserved under deformations. I will begin my presentation with a reminder of what a continuous function is and why it should be generalised. Then, I will introduce the concept of topological spaces, and use the previously developed ideas of continuity to define topological invariants. For example connectedness, Hausdorffness and even some physical invariants. In the end, it will only seem natural that a coffee cup and doughnut are really the same thing.

  4. Evolution of Car Engines in the Japanese Domestic Market

    Michai Vasile

    Over the course of history, humans have been thinking of different ways to travel more efficiently. The most notable change in the way travel is perceived happened during the 20th century, when cars came into global use. They provided the average citizen with a reliable and exciting way of everyday transport. But the technological marvel also came with an economic downside, different in each part of the world. By the end of the 20th century, the Japanese government passed a road tax, which was greatly influenced by the engine displacement, making cars with bigger engines more expensive. In my presentation I will talk about how some Japanese car manufacturers worked their way around this road tax, by squeezing every last drop of power from low-displacement engines, and what innovative methods they used to do so.

  4. A Second Look at the Twin Paradox

  7. Barbour-Bertotti's 'Best-Matching'

    Rishin Madan

    Newtonian mechanics, ever since it was presented in Newton's magnum opus, Principia, has claim to being the most successful scientific theory in humanity's history. Yet, since its inception, there have been those who disagree with Newton's mechanics on a fundamental level, regarding the reference to unobservable spatial structure as inadmissible. This view was most prominently espoused by Gottfried Leibniz and Ernst Mach and is now known as relationalism. However, relationists had not been able to recover the empirical predictions of Newtonian mechanics with just relational distances, leaving them in need of recourse. That is, until 1982, when Julian Barbour and Bruno Bertotti developed their 'best-matching' principle, recovering the same empirical predictions as that of Newtonian mechanics (with caveats), without appeal to absolute structure. In this talk, I will detail Barbour-Bertotti's relational mechanics. I will then leave it to the audience to ponder the relevance and importance of this development.

  8. The problem of induction

    Lasse Wolff

  3. Gödel’s incompleteness theorems

    Ross McDonald

    In 1931, Kurt Gödel published a paper which dashed Hilbert’s hopes to axiomatize all of mathematics and to show its consistency. It emphatically showed that there exist “undecidable statements” within any sufficiently powerful axiomatic system, and further that a formal system cannot be used to prove its own consistency. In this talk I will introduce the major ideas behind Gödel’s proof and briefly discuss an interesting conjecture: that the self-referential structures it introduces could underlie consciousness.

  4. The Astounding Nature of Cellular Automata

    Oskar Maatta

    Cellular automata are generally quite simple models, consisting of a grid of cells, each in one of a finite number of states (e.g. on or off), which evolve in discrete time intervals based on a fixed rule. In this talk I will explore some examples – notably the most famous cellular automaton, Conway’s ‘Game Of Life’, to demonstrate the amazingly complex structures and consequences that can arise from such basic rules. I will then present some real world applications and links to the natural sciences.

  5. Quantum computers: What’s all the fuss about?

    Rayhan Mahmud

    Quantum Computing is a fascinating new field at the intersection of computer science, mathematics and physics. It promises to solve problems which are intractable on classical computers. This field studies how to harness some of the strange aspects of quantum physics for use in computer science, allowing quantum algorithms to decrease the computational steps for some problems by many orders of magnitude. In this talk, I will begin by giving a brief overview of classical computers, addressing some limitations. Next I shall give an introduction to fundamental phenomena in quantum mechanics such as superposition, interference and entanglement which underlie quantum computation. After this I shall look at what qubits are and how different quantum logic gates act on them. I then take a look at examples of some quantum algorithms and the application of quantum computers. The talk then concludes with some comments on limitations and difficulties in building a quantum computer including decoherence.

    Firstly, I will explain why neutrinos were first postulated by Pauli as mystery particles that were necessary to maintain conservation laws.   3. A (very) brief introduction to quantum computing

    Matthew Constable

    I will begin by discussing the history of quantum computing and outline the consequences to society which would result if or when it ever becomes feasible, such as the security of internet communications.   4. False Assumption about Organic Light Emitting Diode Operation, Manufacture and Materials

    Thomas Snell

    I will briefly describe the operation of two types of organic electroluminescent device, OLED and Light Emitting Electrochemical Cell. I will discuss how they are made briefly and why they are made using these methods. Then I will talk about the fact that these two routes of manufacture are used, on the assumption that the materials have low thermal degradation, (at least for polymer based devices.) This statement cannot be made with any confidence this parameter has been measured for <10% of OLED materials. These OLED materials will in fact survive melt processes, briefly discussing how I showed this. Then I will discuss advantages and disadvantages of the melt process, and how it can be improved by using a Light Emitting Electrochemical cell system.

  7. Balancing the Solar System

    Jonathan Burr

    To begin with I'd like to talk about orbital resonance, expand a bit on the theory of it and give some examples (like the gaps in Saturn's rings). I'd also then like to talk about the differences between stable and unstable resonances and possibly its role in allowing larger bodies to eject bodies in a similar orbit out of the solar system. I'd then like to talk about the chaotic nature of the solar system and how models of it tend to fail and talk about the time scales on which that happens.
