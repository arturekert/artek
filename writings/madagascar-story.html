The Island That Sailed Away

How Madagascar drifted off alone — and grew a whole world of animals found nowhere else on Earth

Everything in this story really happened — it just happened very, very slowly, across hundreds of millions of years. Read it aloud together. The little yellow notes are for curious older kids and grown-ups who want the real science words.

Scene 1 · Long, long ago

Everyone squeezed together

Once upon a time, there was no island called Madagascar at all. Its rocks were packed into the middle of an enormous crowd of land, where nearly all the world's continents huddled together in one giant piece.

Africa held India's hand. And little Madagascar was squeezed right in between them, like a child in the middle of a family photo.

Big wordGrown-ups call that giant huddle of land Gondwana — a supercontinent that also held South America, Antarctica and Australia.
Scene 2 · The great crack

The land begins to split

Then, ever so slowly — so slowly that a whole dinosaur could be born, grow old, and turn to stone before anything looked different — the ground began to crack.

Madagascar, still holding India's hand, started pulling away from Africa. Warm seawater came rushing into the gap between them, wider and wider, until there was an ocean where the crack had been.

Big wordThat first sea between Madagascar and Africa is the Mozambique Channel. The splitting began around 180 million years ago.
Scene 3 · A friend lets go

Madagascar is left all alone

For a long while Madagascar and India drifted together, like two friends sharing one small boat. But then the boat split in two!

India let go and hurried off toward the north — in such a rush that one day it would crash into Asia and crumple up the tallest mountains in the world. And Madagascar was left all by itself, a single island in the middle of a wide, wide ocean.

Big wordIndia broke away about 88 million years ago. Madagascar has been an island on its own ever since — that's the secret to everything that comes next.
Scene 4 · The passengers

Whoever was aboard stayed aboard

Here is a funny thing. Whoever happened to be standing on Madagascar when it floated away became its passengers forever.

And back in those days, that meant dinosaurs. A fierce, wrinkle-faced hunter stomped through the forests. A long-necked giant munched the treetops. And there was even a frog so enormous it might have snapped at baby dinosaurs for dinner!

Real namesThe hunter was Majungasaurus, the giant was the titanosaur Rapetosaurus, and the huge frog was Beelzebufo — the "devil frog."
Scene 5 · A quiet island

Goodbye, dinosaurs

Then one terrible day, a rock as big as a mountain fell out of the sky and struck the Earth. All over the world the dinosaurs disappeared — on Madagascar too.

The island grew very quiet. Suddenly there was lots and lots of empty space, and hardly anyone left to fill it. An empty island, waiting…

Big numberThat was 66 million years ago. The same asteroid ended the age of dinosaurs everywhere on Earth.
Scene 6 · The strangest part

Little rafts across the sea

Now here is the strangest part of the whole story. Every so often, far away on the African coast, a storm would tear a tangle of trees and roots loose and wash it out to sea — a little floating raft, with a frightened animal clinging on top.

Most of those tiny sailors were lost to the waves. But once in a very, very long while, a raft drifted all the way across the ocean and bumped into Madagascar. And a small, blinking passenger stepped ashore into an empty land — with the whole island to itself.

One of those little travellers was a furry, big-eyed primate. Its great-great-great-(a-million-greats)-grandchildren became the lemurs. Other rafts carried the ancestors of the spiky tenrecs, the mice, and the catlike fossa.

Big wordScientists call this lucky sea-crossing rafting (or "sweepstakes dispersal"). Lemur ancestors likely arrived roughly 50–60 million years ago.
Scene 7 · A world of their own

They became creatures found nowhere else

With no one else around and a whole island to share, these lucky animals changed and changed and changed — over millions and millions of years — until they became living things you cannot find anywhere else in the whole world.

More than a hundred kinds of lemur, leaping through the trees. Chameleons in every colour, rolling their swivelly eyes. And fat, friendly baobab trees that look as though they were planted upside down, with their roots waving at the sky.

Big wordWhen a plant or animal lives in one place and nowhere else, we say it is endemic. About 9 out of every 10 wild species in Madagascar are endemic.
Scene 8 · Giants and newcomers

Giants — and the last passengers of all

Madagascar even grew giants. The elephant bird stood taller than a grown-up and was heavier than an ox, laying eggs bigger than your head. And some lemurs grew as large as gorillas, climbing slowly through the trees.

Then, only a few thousand years ago — a blink in this long story — the very last new passengers arrived at last: people. And within a short time, sadly, the biggest of the giants were gone forever.

NamesThe giant bird was Aepyornis, the elephant bird. The gorilla-sized climber was the giant lemur Archaeoindris. People reached the island within roughly the last few thousand years.
Scene 9 · Today

Still its own little world

And today? Today Madagascar is still its own little world, floating quietly in the Indian Ocean. Nearly nine out of every ten wild things there — the lemurs, the chameleons, the baobabs, the catlike fossa — live nowhere else on all the Earth.

All of it, every leaping lemur and every upside-down tree, because long, long ago an island went sailing away… and kept its very own secret garden of life.

The End.

The Chronological Outline

Two Stories, Side by Side

What the Earth was doing, and who was living on Madagascar, at each step. Read the blue side for the rocks and continents and the green side for the plants and animals.

The Earth (geology) Life (plants & animals)

The Earth

Madagascar's oldest rocks form deep inside the crust, locked in the heart of a giant supercontinent. No island yet — just a corner of a crowd of joined-up land.
~3 billion – 540 million yrs ago

Life

Only tiny microbes. No plants, no animals on land anywhere in the world yet.

The Earth

Madagascar sits landlocked in the middle of the supercontinent Gondwana, snug between Africa and India. First ice sheets, then warm coal swamps.
~250 million yrs ago

Life

Early reptiles and amphibians, ferns, mossy forests. The same kinds live across all of Gondwana, because the lands are still joined and animals can walk between them.

The Earth

Gondwana cracks apart. Madagascar (carrying India) tears away from Africa and slides south; the sea floods the gap to make the Mozambique Channel.
~180 million yrs ago

Life

Dinosaurs, cycads, conifers and tiny early mammals — still the shared Gondwana crowd, because the split has only just begun.

The Earth

India rips away and races north (later to raise the Himalayas). Now Madagascar is truly alone, an island in the open Indian Ocean.
~88 million yrs ago

Life

Whoever was aboard is now stranded forever: dinosaurs like Majungasaurus and the giant Rapetosaurus, the devil frog Beelzebufo, and the first flowers.

The Earth

A mountain-sized asteroid strikes the planet. Skies darken worldwide — Madagascar included.
66 million yrs ago

Life

The dinosaurs vanish. The island is left quiet and half-empty, with survivors like reptiles, birds and small creatures — and lots of room to spare.

The Earth

Madagascar stays put, ringed by hundreds of kilometres of open sea. Very hard to reach — you can only arrive by luck.
~60 – 20 million yrs ago

Life

New animals raft across the sea on floating mats of vegetation: ancestors of the lemurs, tenrecs, rodents and the fossa. Cut off, they evolve into forms found nowhere else. Baobabs, orchids and chameleons flourish.

The Earth

The island looks much as it does now — highland forests, dry spiny woodlands, and coral coasts.
a few thousand yrs ago

Life

Giants roam: the elephant bird Aepyornis (biggest bird ever), gorilla-sized giant lemurs, and pygmy hippos. Then people arrive — and within a short time the largest animals are gone.

The Earth

Madagascar is the world's 4th-largest island, still slowly drifting — and even, very gently, cracking again along its length.
Today

Life

Over 100 kinds of lemur, chameleons galore, the fossa, and baobabs — and about 90% of its wildlife lives nowhere else on Earth.
